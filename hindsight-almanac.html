The cover story

Yes, you start in Nevada

Why Las Vegas, October 1980

In 1980, legal sports betting existed in exactly one state. Everywhere else it ran illegally through cash bookies, who cap your bet sizes and get to decide whether you actually see a payout after eight straight wins.

Base yourself in Vegas for the betting window. Legal sportsbooks let you spread big bets across several casinos, and there is a bonus most people miss: casino winnings count as legitimate, reportable income. A documented gambling win gives your first pile of cash a clean, boring origin story.

Then leave

After the Super Bowl in January 1981, Nevada has done its job. The money sits in Apple and a brokerage account, and you can live anywhere cheap and pleasant.

One later move matters. Around 2001 you want to be near the Bay Area to run the property play in person, buying San Jose and San Francisco homes at 2000 prices before they post the biggest metro gains in the country. The arc stays simple: Nevada for the seed, the Bay Area for the bricks, anywhere you like for the rest.

The research · property

Where the bricks pay most

No single lucky town wins this. The West Coast does, and the Bay Area leads it, over the 2000 to 2006 window. The catch is a hard exit date.

The 20-year champions

Over the two decades from 2000, the largest metro home-price gains in the country belonged to San Jose (+235%) and San Francisco (+219%). California and Hawaii topped every list, with a cluster of cities clearing a 200 percent rise over their 2000 prices.

Borrowing is what makes property worth including at all. You control a whole house with a small down payment, so a 200 percent gain on the home translates into a far larger return on the cash you actually put down.

The catch: sell by 2006

You cannot simply buy and hold through it. From 2007 the average US home value fell about 33%, nearly wiping out every gain made from 2003 to 2006.

So the plan buys the 2000 dip and sells at the 2006 peak, capturing the borrowed-money run-up and stepping out ahead of the crash. One sober footnote: nationally, housing rose 196 percent since 2000 while the S&P rose 322 percent. Property earns its place through borrowing and diversification, not through raw appreciation.

What this plan is quietly assuming

  1. The sports seed is capped by the bookies. Legal Nevada books set limits, and a bettor who goes eight-for-eight gets flagged fast. Your starting capital is held back by those limits far more than by anything you know.
  2. Every "sell the top, buy the bottom" needs precise recall. This is the assumption carrying the whole plan. Miss the timing and the constant rotation actually underperforms just holding Apple.
  3. Bitcoin custody and liquidity. Millions of early coins are worthless if you lose the key or leave them on Mt. Gox, which lost 850,000 BTC. And selling tens of millions of coins into thin early markets would crash the price against you.
  4. Real estate is illiquid. "Sell everything at the 2006 peak" reads cleaner on a timeline than it plays out when you are trying to offload several mortgaged Bay Area houses inside one window.
  5. The survival problem was only ever real for one winter. You needed a job and a cheap apartment for about six months in Nevada. Past that point the risks were a bookie who wouldn't pay, a lost key, or drawing attention, never the rent.
The fortune was never in knowing what to buy.
